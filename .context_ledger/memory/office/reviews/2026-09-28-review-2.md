## What exists now

Four product commits, all pushed, 295 tests green (up from 223):

- **Wall-clock candles and session levels.** `TimeCandleBuilder` groups ticks into fixed clock intervals,
  and `forex/sessions.py` decides which day a tick belongs to and keeps each closed day's high/low. The
  roll hour is a parameter (`--session-start-hour`) because on a 24-hour FX feed the day boundary is a
  convention, not a fact about the market.
- **The strategy itself** (`forex/trade_strategies.py`): the four lines fixed before the session starts, the
  three-candle sequence at a boundary, the crossing entry, the stop beyond the swing line, the target at the
  opposite range line.
- **A harness that can actually grade it** (`forex/trade_harness.py`, `clicktrader forex-trade-replay`):
  walks every tick between entry and resolution and reports expectancy in R, against the mirror of each
  trade.
- **A new decision type**, `TradePlan` — a direction with a stop and a target, which the old one couldn't
  express. Its entry price is deliberately *not* a field: the harness reads it off the tick the decision was
  made on, so a strategy can't claim an entry it didn't get.

The strategy's docstring lists every place the source blueprint left something open, and what was chosen —
"reaches the zone" means a wick does, the anchor's colour is *not* constrained, the swing lookback is a
parameter because "scroll left" doesn't say how far. It also records what was deliberately **not**
implemented: the blueprint's two "don't be rigid" escape hatches ("if the bottom wicks test an area three
times and hold", "scale out against the biggest seller block"), because neither can be written down without
inventing the rule the source declined to state. That's the same line I drew on the supply-and-demand deck,
applied consistently.

## The part worth reading even if you skip the rest

**The harness was wrong twice before it was right, and both mistakes were in the statistics rather than the
code.**

The verdict was originally going to compare the strategy's absolute expectancy against the mirror's. That
would have been a real defect. While calibrating against a driftless random walk I found that on a tick grid
coarse enough for one tick to jump past a level, a 2:1 trade wins about *half* the time instead of a third —
so the strategy showed **+0.257R** and its mirror **+0.287R**, both positive, neither from skill. Any
edge-shaped number there is an artifact of tick granularity. Comparing two absolute numbers would let that
artifact be read as an edge. The verdict now reads the **paired** difference between each trade and its
mirror, which cancels the shared artifact (it read −0.03R on the same data), and the report prints both
absolute numbers anyway so you can see how much of them is artifact.

The second mistake was a claim, not code: I wrote that the paired comparison "cancels the drift". It does
not — flipping a direction doesn't remove a trend, and the trend is exactly what the comparison then
measures. Found by writing the null test and watching it fail: a single "driftless" 100,000-tick walk still
has a *realized* trend that dwarfs a small barrier, and a long-only plan legitimately beat its own mirror on
it. The test now averages over several realizations, and the docstrings say plainly that **nothing in a
single recording separates "the price went up" from "the method knew it would"** — the mitigations are the
out-of-sample half, a fresh period, and a different instrument. That limit is stated in the product docs, not
just the ledger.

Both are recorded as findings, and the second is in DESIGN.md because it bounds what any verdict on the
forex side can mean.

## It produces zero trades, and that is now a verified fact rather than a prediction

I ran it on the real 61,572-tick EUR/USD capture, and then checked the reason directly rather than assuming
it:

- With a midnight-UTC roll the whole capture is **one session** — no previous session, so no lines at all.
- With a 06:00, 08:00 or 12:00 roll it splits into two, which gives range lines but **no swing lines**,
  because the swing comes from the session *before* the range session and there isn't one.

Zero trades in every configuration. The method needs **three** sessions of data to place its first trade,
and the longest capture here spans one. Everything upstream of that works: I verified 69 wall-clock
15-minute bars built from the real ticks, all aligned to the clock, no spurious gaps, no out-of-order
ticks — and the strategy fires correctly on hand-built sessions in the tests (18 tests covering the pattern,
the trigger, its expiry, the mirror setup, and the roll-hour effect).

So the state is: the machinery is built and verified, the method is implemented as specified, and the one
thing standing between you and an answer is **recording time**. A first trade needs three days of
continuous EUR/USD; a verdict needs ~500 resolved out-of-sample trades on top of that, which at a setup or
two a day is a long capture rather than a long weekend. That second number is why I didn't simply lower the
gate to get a reading: a stop/target outcome is wider-tailed than a coin flip, so it needs *more* trades to
say anything, not fewer.
